# Ability effect model — design rationale

## Background

The ability effect model is a pretrained model of what each card ability does in play, together with the per-ability embeddings that training it produces. The embeddings are the point: downstream model quality has tracked the quality of the pretrained representation more than any other factor, and the current encoders learn from correlations (price, winnability counters) rather than from what abilities actually do. [`2026-05-02-deterministic-feature-reliance.md`](2026-05-02-deterministic-feature-reliance.md) traced the sealed scorer's per-card quality gap to exactly that substitution. The deliverable here is the effect model and its embeddings. Whether those embeddings then improve the scorer, the picker, or the draft agent is a separate later experiment; nothing in this design is justified by downstream win rate.

This document supersedes the training-signal, game-state, and data-collection design in the draft spec [`../specs/2026-XX-XX-game-state-delta-pretraining.md`](../specs/2026-XX-XX-game-state-delta-pretraining.md). The draft's card-representation machinery carries forward: cards as mini-sequences of ability embeddings behind a `[CARD]` token, positional indices that reset at each card, offline embedding caching, raw numeric structured features. Its training signal does not: a hand-designed 20-dimension state-delta vector, averaged over whatever states an ability happened to resolve in, is replaced wholesale below. A dated normative spec will replace the draft once this design settles.

## One factored model serves both the in-game and the deckbuilding use

An effect model is only useful during a game if it conditions on the game state: "destroy all creatures" is worth three cards against a full board and nothing against an empty one. The same model is only useful at deckbuilding or drafting time if it can be queried with no game state at all. One architecture provides both, by forcing everything the model knows about an ability through a fixed-size bottleneck:

```
ability tokens   → ability encoder → e            (one vector per ability line)
(e, game state)  → effect head     → predicted effect
```

The effect head is the in-game model. The bottleneck vector `e` is the state-free embedding, computed offline once per unique ability text and cached, exactly as the draft spec's caching scheme intends. Training against state-conditional targets is also what makes the state-free vector good. To serve the effect head, `e` must encode how the effect varies with state (a sweeper's value scaling with the opposing board), not merely the effect's average. An embedding trained on state-averaged deltas cannot recover that variation; the average is taken before the encoder ever sees a gradient.

The effect head's output lives on the entity tokens. One shared per-entity head, applied to every entity's contextualized vector, predicts what happened to that entity: zone outcome as a categorical wide enough for the real outcomes (stayed, died, exiled, bounced to hand, to library top or bottom, transformed, turned face up or down, phased out, blinked and returned as a new object, countered for stack entities), tapped or untapped, damage taken, counters delta by type, power/toughness delta with its duration, type and color changes with their duration, keywords gained or lost, control change, and attachment change. Attachment is an output field because equipping and enchanting are entire effects whose only observable outcome is the attached-to field. Players are entities too, with their own tokens, and the same head predicts their fields: life delta, cards drawn, discarded, and milled, library events (scried, surveilled, tutored, revealed, reordered), and mana delta. A Bolt to the face is then the same prediction problem as a Bolt to a creature, and player-level continuous effects (can't gain life, skip your untap step) have a home.

One readout remains on the global token: created objects, as a small fixed bank of K slots. A slot predicts a token-script identity over the converted token corpus when the created object is a scripted token (the usual case), and characteristic fields (power/toughness, type flags, keyword flags) for unscripted objects. "Two 1/1 Soldiers" and "one 4/4 Angel" must not collapse into the same count, and a Treasure must be nameable, not just describable.

Two further readouts complete the output surface. The `[ACT]` position carries the playability verdict, the cost paid, and the trigger-fired bit; questions about the acting ability read out where the acting ability sits. The per-entity head gains a legality field group that playability records supervise. Target legality is relative to the acting ability in `[ACT]`. Blocker legality is pairwise in the rules, so it is anchored: one blocker-legality record per candidate attacker, the attacker marked with the existing source flag and `[ACT]` left empty, the record-kind flag disambiguating, with per-entity legal-blocker bits relative to that attacker. Attacker legality is absolute, carried in one combat-setup record per decision point. Cardinality constraints like menace are assignment-level spec work. A legal-blocker set is a per-entity predicate, and it is where evasion keywords get their gradient.

Identity stays in the loss this way: "destroy all creatures with power 4 or greater" trains a pointwise predicate on every entity rather than an aggregate count, and every "nothing happened to this one" is an informative label. The predictions vary in number with the board; the parameters do not, because one head is mapped over the entities, the same shape as a masked-token head. The head is hierarchical, the standard treatment for sparse targets: a per-entity affected/unaffected gate first, conditional field predictions only where the gate fires. The created-objects readout is a set prediction, and whether its loss matches slots to objects or imposes a canonical order is spec work. The ability encoder pools through a `[CLS]`-style aggregation token, as the draft spec specifies; effect-head gradient reaches that token directly, which is what keeps an aggregation token from degenerating. A small autoregressive event decoder stays available as an upgrade if the per-entity fields prove too coarse; the corpus stores full event lists either way, so switching output structure needs no re-collection.

## No single signal covers the corpus — abilities route to collectors by runtime trait kind

Static abilities never resolve, so a per-resolution signal gives the corpus's largest ability-line kind zero training examples. The converted corpus (32,606 files, one per card name; 33,524 face blocks) breaks down by line kind as follows; `name:`, `types:`, and metadata lines (`mana cost:`, `power toughness:`, `layout:`, `loyalty:`, `defense:`, `text:`, `draft:`) are excluded:

| line kind         |  lines | share | dominant record kind                   |
|-------------------|-------:|------:|----------------------------------------|
| `static`          | 18,163 |   29% | continuous-effect records              |
| `triggered`       | 16,694 |   26% | resolution + trigger-fire records      |
| `activated`       | 11,088 |   17% | resolution records                     |
| `spell`           |  8,540 |   13% | resolution records                     |
| `replacement`     |  3,286 |    5% | rewrite records                        |
| `option`          |  1,896 |    3% | resolution records (as the chosen mode)|
| `alternate cost`  |  1,279 |    2% | playability + cost records             |
| `planeswalker`    |    942 |    1% | resolution records                     |
| `additional cost` |    844 |    1% | playability + cost records             |
| `chapter`         |    555 |    1% | resolution records                     |
| `cost reduction`  |    251 |   <1% | playability records                    |
| `level`           |    111 |   <1% | continuous-effect records              |

Within the `static:` mass, repetition dominates: nearly half the lines are bare single-word keywords (8,838, with `flying` alone at 3,155), and only 4,943 static texts are unique. The statics problem is therefore mostly a keywords problem, and its behavioral signal comes from combat and legality rather than from anything a fork must construct.

Routing happens at runtime, by trait kind: a resolving spell, activated ability, or trigger produces a resolution record, a replacement application produces a rewrite record, a continuous static produces continuous-effect records, and combat produces combat records. The converted line is the join target for those records, not the router. A rendered line can bundle several runtime traits, so one line can legitimately receive records from more than one collector: Glorybringer's exert converts as a single `static:` line, but its observable effect is a trigger resolution, and its records are resolution records. The table is therefore an accounting of the corpus by rendered line kind with each kind's dominant record kind, and the provenance sidecar carries the exact line-to-trait mapping.

Three further record kinds fall out of machinery that already runs. Combat records cover what no ability line carries (printed power and toughness) and double as the observational bulk for the damage-step keywords; the evasion family gets its signal from playability records instead, since combat outcomes given declared blocks carry no flying gradient. Trigger-fire records supervise when a trigger fires, which resolution records alone never teach: the trigger handler already evaluates every active trigger's condition against every fired event, so logging (trigger line, event, state) with a fired-or-not label costs no extra simulation and comes with natural negatives. The negatives are subsampled to same-event-type pairs, so they are not trivially unrelated.

Playability records supervise what the rules allowed and at what price. At every decision point the AI already computes its candidate list with rejection reasons (cannot afford, no legal target, cannot play now), the legal-target sets, and the legal attackers and blockers, and the legality and cost-adjustment checks know which static forbade a block or changed a cost. Logging all of it is one line at calls that already run. Playability records are the only signal for costs, timing restrictions, summoning sickness, and the targeting restrictions of abilities that were never activated, and the cost-kind lines in the table join to them and to the cost half of activations.

Probe records shrink to the residual that observation cannot reach. All collectors share one typed event vocabulary and one game-state format, so the corpus is one schema with seven record kinds, not seven corpora. A resolution record is a linked pair (the cost record at activation, the effect record at resolution) counted as one kind. Every record kind snapshots its state context pre-event. Every record also names its kind and moment through a flag on the global token, because different kinds can present near-identical states while asking different questions.

## Resolution records: typed, attributed events replace the hand-designed delta vector

The draft's snapshot-diff design (record aggregate state before and after resolution, subtract) is replaced by attributed event logging: during a resolution, Forge knows which `SpellAbility` is resolving, so the worker records the discrete engine events that resolution causes: this creature was destroyed, that player drew two cards, this permanent became tapped. Three defects of the snapshot diff motivated the change:

- A diff loses object identity. "Opponent creatures −2" cannot say which two creatures died, so a conditional removal ("destroy all creatures with power 4 or greater") is indistinguishable from a size-2 sweep of the weakest bodies. An event list names the affected objects, and the affected objects sit in the state context, so conditionality becomes learnable.
- A diff is contaminated. Between the two snapshots, state-based actions fire and other triggers land on the stack; the diff credits all of it to the resolving ability. An attributed event is credited to its actual source.
- A diff cannot express a rewrite. The replacement-effect signal (next section) needs events as its native vocabulary anyway; using events everywhere makes all the record kinds share one target language.

A resolution record carries the ability-line reference, the state context, the chosen targets and modes, the announced values (X, kicker and other optional costs paid), and the attributed event list. Targets and announced values are part of the record because effect prediction is ill-posed without them: the same Lightning Bolt kills a creature or halves a life total depending on where it was pointed, and Fireball's effect is a function of the X its caster announced. Targets are marked as a flag on the targeted entities in the state context, and a modal spell's record attributes to the chosen `option` line rather than the parent `spell` line. Resolution-time choices the engine makes while resolving (the creature picked for an edict, search results, a chosen creature type) are recorded as inputs alongside targets and X, so the head conditions on the choice instead of having to predict Forge's policy.

The cost record carries a resolution outcome (resolved, fizzled, partially fizzled, declined, countered). A "you may" the AI declines is then distinguishable from an ability that genuinely did nothing, and a countered spell is a cost record with that outcome, not a separate record kind. Declined and countered are response and policy outcomes rather than rules outcomes, so the model conditions on them instead of predicting them. Attribution granularity is the sub-ability: Forge resolves a sub-ability chain as distinct objects with their own API types, so events attribute to the resolving link, the sidecar maps links to lines, and the root line is the fallback. Events that set up continuing effects carry a duration field sourced from the effect's parameters, so an until-end-of-turn pump and a permanent change are different targets, not the same stat delta.

Activation and resolution are two moments, and the record structure respects that. Paying costs happens at activation; the effect happens at resolution, possibly after responses. Each activation therefore writes a cost record (mana removed from the pool by color, permanents tapped, life paid, sacrifices) linked by id to the eventual resolution record. A countered or fizzled spell leaves a cost record whose outcome field says so, with no resolution partner; the missing partner is genuine signal rather than a gap. Paying three mana for nothing is a real game effect.

Resolution records are observational plus interventional. Observation only covers abilities where Forge chose to use them: a sweeper never resolves on an empty board, a burn spell is never pointed at the target the AI rejected, and a card the AI never casts (a documented Forge-AI failure category) gets no records at all. Forge's own simulation API closes the gap: `GameSimulator.simulateSpellAbility(sa, resolve)` copies the game and force-resolves an ability with chosen targets, so the collector can also record counterfactual resolutions (other targets, other modes, abilities the AI declined) on forked copies. Interventional records are flagged in the corpus metadata only. The flag is never a model input, because the model would partition its function across the flag and the interventions would stop transferring. Each intervention costs one game copy and runs under the same per-game budget discipline as probes.

Four facts shape interventional records. The record stores the fork's own state rather than the live game's, because the copier drops the stack and most this-turn history. A card the AI cannot afford is forced through forge-game's play-without-paying-mana path, because the simulator's own cost payment refuses with a sentinel score. The simulator locates the ability on the copy by matching description text with a prefix fallback, so interventions on modal spells and same-text abilities verify the match through the provenance key instead. And force-resolving drains the whole stack on the fork, so fork records attribute by bracket like everything else.

Two known approximations are accepted. Deaths from lethal damage happen in the state-based-action pass immediately after resolution, not during it. Attribution uses the bracket: state-based actions also run before any player receives priority, so nothing is pending when a resolution starts, and the pass that follows a resolution bracket belongs to that resolution. The rule is exact up to replacement and static interactions inside the same window, the corner case already accepted, and combat damage uses the combat damage step as its bracket in the same way. Separately, mana abilities never reach the stack (Forge resolves them inline on activation), but the inline path still fires the cast and resolution triggers with the ability in the parameters, so mana records ride the trigger channel with no extra hook. The bus-only stage cannot see them, because that path emits only a log string, so mana records begin when the patch lands. Mana abilities are also the most frequent event in any game and nearly deterministic, so they are capped per unique ability text, or they dominate the corpus.

## Rewrite records: replacement effects are directly observable, one application at a time

A replacement effect's whole job is to turn one event into another, and Forge exposes exactly that. The rules (CR 616) apply simultaneously-applicable replacements one at a time, the affected player choosing the order, and Forge's `ReplacementHandler` implements this faithfully: `run()` selects a single replacement, executes it against the event's parameter map, then recursively re-runs the handler on the modified event for whatever still applies. Replacements are never applied as a batch, so hooking the single-application point yields one record per replacement per event: the replacement's line reference, the incoming event, the outgoing event.

Stacked replacements attribute exactly. Hardened Scales plus Doubling Season on "put one +1/+1 counter" produces two records: Hardened Scales rewrote one counter to two, and Doubling Season rewrote two to four. Each record is a true observation of that replacement's rewrite function on the event it actually received. Order dependence (Doubling Season first yields 1 → 2 → 3 instead) is not a confound; a replacement is by definition a function of its incoming event, and different orders simply supply different incoming events. Damage prevention and redirection run through a second, non-recursive pipeline in the same handler. Both pipelines pass through one shared execution point (private, so this is one of the three patched hooks), and the hook deep-copies the parameter map before the call, because the handler's own copy is shallow and replacements mutate nested structures in place.

The training task for a rewrite record is to predict the outgoing event from the replacement's embedding, the incoming event, and the state context; the outgoing event is expressed through the same per-entity outputs (under Doubling Season, the creature carrying "incoming: CounterAdded, n=1" gets a predicted counters delta of two). The incoming event enters the model as a pending-event overlay on the entities it affects (the creature about to receive counters carries "incoming: CounterAdded, n=1"), so events-as-inputs reuse the entity machinery rather than needing a separate encoder. A rewrite whose outgoing event creates objects (a token-doubling replacement) routes its prediction through the created-objects readout, since the new objects have no entity to carry an overlay. Rewrite records take the replacement controller's perspective. Replacement text is controller-relative ("if one or more counters would be put on a permanent you control…"), so active-player framing would flip the mine/opponent tags against the text's "you". Event-modifying cards therefore need no perturbation probe: Doubling Season's converted line is a `replacement:` line, and its rewrite records already carry its full effect.

## Continuous-effect records: the layer system already attributes statics

A static ability's ongoing effect does not need a counterfactual fork to be observed, because Forge's layer system records which static changed which entity, and how. Every continuous change a static applies (power/toughness boosts, keyword changes, type and color changes) is written into per-card tables keyed by (timestamp, static id), and the continuous-effects pass stamps each entry with the applying static's id. A continuous-effect record is read straight out of those tables after a recompute: source static, affected entities, and the exact per-entity contribution, coalesced per (game, static, board state) so a stable anthem yields one record per board rather than one per recompute. The acting static's own contribution is subtracted from the input overlay for its own records: every layer-table channel it wrote is removed (boosts, granted keywords, type, color, and name changes), so the task is to predict what the static contributes to a board it is not yet applying to. Without that masking the target is readable off the overlay's boost channel, the head learns to copy input rather than read text, and the held-out numbers on the corpus's largest line kind pass vacuously. Restriction and cost statics, which change no characteristic, are covered by playability records instead: the legality and cost-adjustment checks evaluate with the responsible static in scope, so "this static forbade this block" and "this static changed this cost" log with natural negatives and no game copy.

Two source caveats shape the collector. The layer tables' column key is the static's id, and the id accessor is public, so the join is a direct id read (the applied-effect object's own ability reference is private, which does not matter). And temporary pumps write static id zero into the same tables, so until-end-of-turn effects stay attributed by the resolution bracket, not the layer tables.

## Probe records: the damage step is the one place forks are still needed

First strike, double strike, deathtouch, lifelink, trample, indestructible, wither, and infect change no legal action and no computed characteristic; their entire effect lives in what the combat damage step does. Combat records already exhibit those outcomes per entity, with the keyword sitting on the participant, so the probe is contingent: it is built only if the observational canary fails, meaning combat-trained embeddings do not separate the damage-step keywords and the fork must supply the isolated counterfactual. The probe, if needed: fork at declare-blockers after blocks are locked in, strip one keyword from one participant, and resolve the damage step on the fork. The fork branch is trained as an ordinary combat record on the perturbed state, carrying the combat kind flag; fork provenance stays corpus metadata, like the interventional flag. The diff against the real branch is an evaluation-side construct, never a training target, because a diff of a categorical zone outcome is not a value the shared head can emit. Both branches consume the AI's damage-assignment decisions, so both are seeded identically: Forge's global random source cannot be snapshotted, and the engine's own pattern is to install a seeded generator and restore the reference afterward. One copy, no turns played forward.

Probes, when built, run under a per-game budget since each costs a game-state copy. Rollout diffing stays available as a later extension for anything the damage step, the layer tables, and the playability hooks all miss, but it is not the baseline.

Printed power and toughness need no probe. P/T is not an ability and gets no embedding (it stays a raw numeric structured feature, as the draft spec already decided), and the regularity a P/T edit probe would teach is the densest fact in the corpus: every combat record and every damage event exhibits lethal damage killing and bigger bodies winning trades. Probe copies are spent on keywords instead.

## Combat records: the observational complement, nearly free

Every combat already generates attributed engine events (attacks declared, blocks assigned, damage dealt, deaths), so logging them costs no probes and no new corpus machinery. A combat record carries the declared attackers and blockers, the participants' state, and the damage-step outcome, with no ability slot. The training task predicts the outcome given the declared combat, which is rules mechanism; it never predicts the block assignments themselves, which are Forge's policy and not the modeling target. Damage-assignment choices (which blocker takes lethal damage, trample ordering) are policy too, so they are recorded as inputs exactly like resolution-time choices, and the target is the outcome given declaration and assignment. Gradient reaches the embeddings through the participants' entity representations rather than through an actor.

Combat records supervise the damage-step keyword family (first strike, deathtouch, lifelink, trample, indestructible), not the evasion family. Given declared blocks, flying changes nothing about the outcome, so flying is an input-only feature here with no discriminative gradient. Evasion's signal is the legal-blocker sets in playability records: flying's embedding is shaped by every block that was or wasn't legal against a flyer across the corpus, logged by the playability collector. Printed power and toughness earn their observational meaning here in the damage-step outcomes, alongside the damage-step keywords.

## Playability records: what the rules allowed, observed at decision points

Effect records teach what an ability does when used; nothing in them teaches when it may be used or what it costs, because an ability the AI never activates produces no record at all. Playability records close that gap for free. At every decision point the AI computes a candidate list with per-candidate verdicts and rejection reasons (cannot afford, no legal target, cannot play now), plus the legal-target sets; combat setup computes the legal attackers and blockers; and the legality and cost-adjustment checks evaluate with the responsible static in scope. Logging those, with the verdict and the responsible static where one applies, is one line at calls that already run, with no fork and no extra simulation.

The logged verdicts are the rules-level checks only: can-play, cost payability, legal-target enumeration. The AI's decision enum mixes rules with policy (cannot-afford sits next to "another time" and "life in danger"), and training on the policy half would teach Forge's opinions rather than the rules. Two engine hazards shape the collector: the AI's evaluation loop can be killed mid-verdict by its timeout thread, and the per-ability legality check installs targets as a side effect, so the logger snapshots defensively and never reuses the checked ability object. The training task predicts the rules verdict, the cost paid, and the legality sets from (`e`, state), which is also the only direct supervision the cost side of an ability gets for spells that stay in hand.

## Game state is a set of entity tokens that reuse the ability embeddings

The draft spec's `GameStateSnapshot` reduces every creature to power/toughness plus a keyword enum and every non-creature permanent to a count, which makes a 2/2 with "sacrifice: counter target spell" identical to Grizzly Bears and an opposing Doubling Season invisible. The replacement is the entity-based state encoding that game-playing systems settled on (AlphaStar — Vinyals et al. 2019; OpenAI Five — Berner et al. 2019, [arXiv:1912.06680](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06680)): the state is a variable-length set of entity tokens processed by a permutation-invariant transformer, which is already this project's house architecture for pools.

Each battlefield entity reuses the card representation the rest of the pipeline is built on: a `[CARD]`-style token carrying structured features plus a dynamic overlay, followed by the card's ability-embedding tokens. The overlay carries current power/toughness decomposed into base, continuous boosts, and counter contributions (Forge exposes the breakdown directly, so an anthem is visible as a boost rather than folded into one number), tapped and summoning-sickness state, damage marked, counters by type, combat status (attacking, and blocking or blocked by whom), what it is attached to, temporarily granted keywords and abilities, a face-down flag, and two role flags: targeted by the resolving ability, and source of the resolving ability. Granted keywords are read from the timestamped change tables, because "gains flying until end of turn" is otherwise invisible; the ability list shows printed text and attachments only.

Entity identity is a current-characteristics reference (card name, face, copy-source), never a bare name: a Clone, a morph, or a transformed face otherwise becomes an entity carrying the wrong abilities. The `[CARD]` structured features carry the full type line explicitly (card types, supertypes, and subtypes via the draft spec's subtype-token mean), colors, and mana value. Most effect predicates quantify over exactly these ("nonblack creature", "artifact or enchantment", "Goblin you control"), and the `types:` line is excluded from the ability lines, so nothing else provides them. The source flag matters because much of the corpus references its own source ("CARDNAME gets +1/+1", "sacrifice CARDNAME"); the resolving ability's host is always present as an entity, as a stack entity when it is a spell being cast, so its characteristics are readable even mid-cast. Entities are controller-tagged (mine vs opponent's) from the perspective of the resolving ability's controller, the same controller-relative framing the draft spec used.

Reuse closes the loop: the effect head evaluates an ability against a board described in the same embedding space it is training, so gradient reaches every embedding from both its actor role and its context role. Five details follow from taking the game's own semantics seriously:

- Snapshot computed characteristics, not printed ones. Forge has already applied the layer system, so an anthem's effect is visible in every affected entity's overlay.
- Attachments contribute their granted abilities to the host's ability-token list, which is literally what an aura does.
- Creature tokens and other token objects use converted token scripts: Forge's 802 token scripts are converted alongside the card corpus, so a Treasure on the battlefield is an entity with its sacrifice ability's `e`, and the created-objects readout can name token identities instead of guessing at characteristics.
- Player-scoped continuous effects with no permanent ("you can't gain life this turn") exist in Forge as command-zone effect cards; those appear as entities carrying the originating ability's `e`.
- Each player is an entity with a token of its own, carrying life total, zone sizes (hand, library, graveyard), poison and energy, the this-turn counters that common conditions read (creatures that died, spells cast, lands played), the mana state (floating mana and untapped production by color), and the same targeted flag permanents have. "Any target" is then uniform across creatures, players, and planeswalkers. The global token keeps only what belongs to the game rather than to a player: turn, phase, whose turn, priority, stack size, the combat sub-step (first-strike vs regular damage), and command-zone emblems. Without per-color mana state, "add {R}" has no visible consequence for the effect head to predict. Stack entities carry their own target references (a counterspell that cares what its target targets reads them), and announced per-target amounts (divided damage) and up-to-N counts are explicit inputs. Combat records, the one kind with no actor, fix their perspective as the active player's; every other kind takes its acting or owning controller's perspective.

Fidelity is tiered for unreferenced context only: any object a record references (targets, affected objects, the source) is always included as an entity with a zone attribute, whatever zone it sits in, because otherwise counterspells, discard, and reanimation would have records with no entities to predict on. Beyond that, the global token plus battlefield entities are the core tier; unreferenced stack contents (what a response is responding to) come next; unreferenced hand and graveyard contents are later tiers.

One collection principle outranks every representation choice above. The corpus stores state as data (card names plus dynamic attributes per entity), and the tensor representation is derived at training time. Every future representation change is then a code change, never a re-collection. The draft spec baked the feature vector into the corpus, which would have frozen the state design at collection time.

## New keywords are handled by expansion dropout over their definitions

A keyword token with a free learned embedding gives a new keyword nothing: it tokenizes to `[UNK]` until the stack is retrained. The fix is keyword-expansion dropout: during training, a keyword token is replaced by its definition text with probability p, so the model learns the compact token and the expanded form as the same thing; at zero-shot time, an unknown keyword is always expanded. The objections to inline expansion (long sequences, a shifted token distribution, lost compact tokens) assumed expansion was total and permanent; probabilistic expansion keeps sequences short in the common case, reserves full expansion for unknowns, and the augmentation itself regularizes. Recursion needs no special rule: keywords inside an expansion stay tokens, themselves subject to the same dropout on other samples. (The dictionary-embedding line of work, Bahdanau et al. 2017 ([arXiv:1706.00286](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.00286)) and Dict2vec (Tissier et al. 2017), solves the same problem by computing the token's embedding from its definition; the rejected-alternatives section says why dropout-expansion won here.)

The definition text is Forge's per-keyword reminder-text template at first: it is prose, the language the encoder speaks; it lives on the keyword enum with no card needed; and it covers every keyword, including the engine-coded minority (first strike, trample, haste) that generates no script at all. The desugared implementations (the triggers, statics, and replacements Forge generates for roughly 150 of 199 keywords) are script DSL, captured as text at the point CardFactoryUtil builds them, so they become first-class definitions once the script surface lands at stage four. A keyword only exists in Forge once someone has implemented it, so either way a new set's variation-keyword arrives with a definition on day zero. Definitions reference other keywords (flying's names reach, and reach's names flying); under expansion dropout that needs no handling, since a referenced keyword is just a token that may or may not expand on some other sample.

Definitions handle day zero; behavioral data handles the ground truth. Ward's strongest semantic neighbors are the cards that spell out "whenever this becomes the target of a spell an opponent controls, counter it unless they pay {2}" without saying ward. Text similarity alone cannot find them; the surface forms barely overlap. Their rewrite and resolution records are nearly identical, and that is what pulls them together in embedding space. The two mechanisms cover each other's blind spots, so both are in the design. Retraining, when it is ever needed, is contained by the bottleneck: downstream consumers read the fixed-width `e`, so a fine-tune of the ability encoder plus a cache refresh never moves the interface.

## Mana symbols: the script separates roles structurally, and supervision provides the polarity

Earlier encoders could not tell `{R}` in a cost from `{R}` in a mana ability's effect, and nothing in their training signal ever forced the distinction. The fix has an input half and a supervision half.

Input half: on the script surface the ambiguity does not exist, because cost is a separate structured parameter (`Cost$ W T`). The boundary is syntax there, not something a small model reconstructs from a comma and a colon. The prose surface keeps the ambiguity the converter created by flattening those fields, so prose tokens carry a role tag per token (cost, effect, trigger-condition, target-spec) with a small learned role embedding added to each token embedding, the segment-embedding mechanism from BERT (Devlin et al. 2018); this is a secondary detail now that script is primary. Role embeddings beat splitting the vocabulary into `pay{R}`/`add{R}` variants because the two uses share one color identity: affinity transfers across roles, and only the polarity rides the role channel.

Supervision half: the corpus observes the two roles with opposite signs. An activation's cost record contains "mana pool −{W}, source became tapped"; a mana ability's resolution record contains "mana pool +{R}". The same symbol appears thousands of times with role-dependent consequences, so the model cannot conflate the roles without contradicting its training data. `{T}` needs no special handling at all once the state is entity-based: tapping is a visible overlay change on the source entity, and the activation's cost record captures it.

## Coverage and memorization are separate tail problems, and both get levers

Most unique ability texts occur exactly once in the corpus (roughly 89%: 32,477 of 36,514 unique line texts, across 63,649 lines), and coverage is far worse than deck-slot arithmetic suggests. The coverage numbers were verified against the gen-4 embedding corpus (974,028 games), with double-faced names split on their separator so combined win-rate rows join to per-face converted files. 4,626 of 32,606 converted files (14.2%) never appear in any deck, because pool generation samples sealed-legal sets and cards outside the rotation are unreachable by construction. Another 2,504 covered cards are never cast. Roughly a fifth of all converted files (21.9%) therefore have zero possible resolution records. Among the 24,790 converted files hosting at least one singleton text (the file, one per card name, is the coverage unit), the median is 112 casts, just under half sit below 100, and a quarter sit at zero.

Piggybacking on match generation is therefore necessary but not sufficient, and interventional forks cannot reach a card that is in no game. The fix is a coverage collector: self-play whose decks are built to exercise uncovered cards, drawn from the whole converted corpus rather than sealed-legal sets. The effect model does not care whether the deck is a plausible sealed deck, only that the card reaches play. For the in-deck-but-never-cast slice, coverage decks only create the precondition, because Forge still declines to cast those cards; deck construction therefore consults castability to spend slots on cards Forge will actually play, and the residue falls to forced interventions.

The other tail problem is memorization, and it is prevented in training, not merely measured in evaluation. For well-covered texts the effect head can treat `e` as a lookup key and store per-ability behavior in its own weights, and in-distribution held-out numbers then look excellent whether or not the encoder reads text; the masked-token auxiliary does not prevent this. Four levers, all cheap: `e` stays small with additive noise during training, so storing a per-text key is expensive; context ability tokens are randomly dropped, so the head cannot rely on a full-board lookup; number tokens get a monotone numeric embedding (a learned vector plus a scalar projection), so magnitudes extrapolate instead of being memorized per integer; and the identity-only baseline in the evaluation is gating. The encoder still trains from random initialization (the sealed encoder's weights carry the winnability correlations this design replaces, the argument spec 05-03 already made), the masked-token auxiliary stays as a cheap regularizer, and the held-out split is card-disjoint by newest sets; the evaluation section carries the details.

## The script is the primary surface; prose is the paired secondary

The converted prose line is not derived from the card's mechanism: it is the hand-written description text from the Forge script (Glorybringer's converted exert line is its static's `Description$` field verbatim). The script is the mechanism: `Cost$`, `ValidTgts$`, `Mode$`, `NumDmg$` are the predicate structure every effect record supervises. The ability encoder therefore trains on the script as the primary surface, with converted prose as the paired secondary and a paired-encoding loss pulling the two `e` vectors of one line together. The pairing loss is asymmetric (a stop-gradient on the script side, or a predictor head on the prose side): a symmetric pull adds uniform collapse pressure on `e`, and the small-noisy-`e` memorization lever would amplify that pressure rather than resist it.

Script-primary buys four things at once. Deployment needs nothing else, because every consumer of `e` evaluates real Forge cards, which have scripts on day zero. The desugared keyword definitions become directly encodable, captured as text where they are generated. Different wordings collapse to the same mechanism (a hundred paraphrases of "deal 3 damage to any target" are all `SP$ DealDamage | NumDmg$ 3 | ValidTgts$ Any`), which is exactly the equivalence class the effect model wants. And it unlocks synthetic script variants, the strongest anti-memorization and numeric-extrapolation lever available: perturb a script parameter (`NumDmg$ 3 → 2`, `ValidTgts$ Creature → Creature.OppCtrl`), load the variant as a custom card, and play or fork it for engine-ground-truth records of a text that never existed, with no prose to write. The costs are a script tokenizer that splits compound selectors (`Creature.nonDragon+OppCtrl`) compositionally and the vocabulary rebuild, both paid at the stage the build order already carries. Prose stays as the paired surface rather than being dropped, because hypothetical cards with no script remain a possible consumer and the pairing itself regularizes; until the rebuild lands, the classification head from `e` to the script's API type plus coarse parameters stands in, at a small weight so behavioral records dominate the embedding geometry.

## Training mechanics

Count-valued targets with open-ended magnitudes (damage amounts, counters, cards drawn, life) get count-regression losses in the Poisson family. Categorical heads are reserved for closed vocabularies (zone outcome), because a categorical over magnitudes cannot emit values outside its trained support and would decide the numeric-extrapolation held-out by construction. The per-entity targets are sparse, so training starts with the dense fields (zone outcome, damage, power/toughness, per-player life and cards) and adds keywords, control, duration, and attachment once those train.

The expensive part of a batch is the context: gradient reaching embeddings through their context role means re-encoding every ability on the board, and boards are dominated by repeats (basic-land mana abilities, and `flying` alone accounts for over three thousand lines). Batches therefore mix several games, group each game's records together so grouped records share boards, and encode each unique ability text once per batch. If that still exceeds the 8 GB GPU budget, the fallback is a stop-gradient cache of context embeddings refreshed on a schedule, with live gradient only through the acting ability (the momentum-encoder pattern from contrastive learning). Validation uses two splits because they answer different questions: a card-disjoint-by-newest-sets split measures generalization to unseen cards (the evaluation section says why ability-disjoint and naive set-disjoint both leak), and a game-disjoint split measures in-distribution fit and catches leakage, since records from one game share a board and are heavily correlated.

The corpus stores per-record state snapshots rather than a replayable per-game event log. A log would be far smaller, but it makes event-vocabulary completeness load-bearing (any state change missing from the stream corrupts every later reconstruction) and adds a replay step that itself needs verification. A snapshot at fifteen to thirty entities with a dozen attributes each is a few hundred bytes compressed, and delta-encoding within a game is the middle ground if size ever pinches.

Collection piggybacks on the existing match generation: instrumenting the match-outcomes workers makes every self-play match feed the effect corpus at no extra simulation cost, and the coverage collector supplies the cards match play can never reach. Sampling design is settled in the spec up front, not left as tuning: a million games yield on the order of a hundred million records dominated by mana taps and routine combat, and the 8 GB training budget sees a small fraction of them, so the mixture over record kinds and importance sampling by ability rarity decide what the model actually learns. Rarity is counted in effective games per unique text, not records: rare-text records are heavily within-game correlated (a coalesced anthem, a playability record repeated per decision point), so record-counting would replay the same boards and overstate tail coverage.

## Rejected and deferred alternatives

- **Repairing the snapshot-delta draft in place** (keep the 20-dim delta for stack abilities, add probes for statics, feed state in). Rejected: it repairs coverage but keeps the lossy hand-designed target and the diff contamination, which were the primary dissatisfactions, and replacements still get nothing.
- **A full world model** (serialize the whole state and train (state, ability) → next state as sequence prediction). Deferred, not rejected: it is the natural architecture for eventually learning to *play*, but it spends most of its capacity copying the large untouched share of the state, and the event-list target used here is the same objective with the unchanged state factored out. The embeddings-first lesson from earlier models says to build the representation now and the world model later, on top of it.
- **17lands data as the signal source.** Rejected: no engine internals (no events, no rewrites, no forks), and its human-play distribution is off-target for a pipeline whose objective is Forge-piloted play.
- **Total inline keyword expansion** (the draft spec's §3.2: replace every keyword with its rules text, everywhere and permanently). Rejected in favor of keyword-expansion dropout: total expansion buys the same zero-shot property at the price of long sequences, a changed vocabulary distribution, and the loss of the compact, heavily-trained keyword tokens; the probabilistic form keeps the property and drops those costs.
- **Split pay/add mana vocabulary.** Rejected for the role channel, as above: split tokens sever the shared color identity and double the mana-symbol vocabulary.
- **Retrofitting** (Faruqui et al. 2015: post-hoc nudging embeddings toward a lexicon). Rejected: it presumes pretrained embeddings to nudge and a synonym-graph-shaped lexicon; the keyword lexicon here is definitional and small.
- **A ±1 printed-P/T edit probe.** Rejected: the regularity it would teach is the densest fact in the corpus (every combat record and damage event exhibits lethal damage killing), so probe copies go to keywords instead.
- **Per-event-type count and magnitude heads** (a fixed histogram over event types per record). Rejected as the primary output: a count of Destroyed events is "opponent creatures −2" again, so the object identity that motivated events over deltas would never reach the loss. Counts survive only in the created-objects readout, the one genuinely aggregate outcome.
- **Option-space fork probes** (fork the game, strip a static, diff legal actions and characteristics). Superseded: the layer tables attribute every continuous change to its static observationally, and the legality and cost hooks log what a static forbade with natural negatives, so the same information arrives without game copies. Forks remain only for the damage-step keywords and interventional resolutions.
- **A replayable per-game event log as the corpus format.** Rejected in favor of per-record snapshots: replay makes event-vocabulary completeness load-bearing and adds a reconstruction step to verify, and snapshot sizes are manageable with within-game delta-encoding in reserve.
- **Definition-computed keyword embeddings** (the keyword token's input vector computed from its definition through the encoder, after Bahdanau et al. 2017). Superseded by keyword-expansion dropout: the same zero-shot behavior with no computed/free blending and no recursion rule, and the expansion doubles as augmentation.
- **Converted prose as the primary encoding surface.** Superseded by script-primary: the prose is the script's hand-written description, not the mechanism; every deployment consumer sees real cards with scripts; and synthetic variants and captured keyword definitions exist only on the script side. Prose remains as the paired secondary surface.
- **The AI's play verdict as the playability label.** Rejected: the decision enum mixes rules-level checks with policy judgments, and the policy half would train the head on Forge's opinions rather than the rules.

## Evaluation adds behavioral checks to the draft's intrinsic ones

The draft spec's intrinsic checks carry forward: nearest-neighbor inspection, UMAP cluster maps colored by effect category, encoder-collapse canaries. The effect corpus adds checks the draft could not express:

- Held-out effect prediction, per record kind, on a card-disjoint split: the held-out unit is the card across all its printings, chosen as the cards first printed in the newest sets (the deployment case). Masking from training-time context means the strongest form: every game containing a held-out card is excluded from training entirely, which is exactly the deployment situation of a model trained before the set existed. An ability-disjoint split leaks (a held-out ability's `e` still receives gradient through its context role and the text auxiliaries), and a naive set-disjoint split leaks through reprints. Results stratify by how much of a card's text is unique to it, with two extra held-outs: novel combinations of seen sub-abilities, and numeric extrapolation (seen effect, unseen magnitude). Metrics condition on affected entities and balance per field, because most entities are unaffected by most records and an unconditioned average sits near ceiling for any model. Every record kind also reports a state-only floor (`[ACT]` and context `e` masked) so text contribution is separated from state predictability.
- Two baselines bound what the encoder contributes, and the first is gating, measured where it is strong: on the card-disjoint split's unique-text stratum, with a margin the spec pins, the text encoder must beat an identity-only baseline (a free embedding per unique text, no text encoder). The stratum matters because on novel texts that baseline degenerates to state-plus-noise, so an unconditioned comparison would pass vacuously. The same identity-only baseline is additionally reported on the game-disjoint split, where it is the memorization ceiling the encoder should approach in-distribution. A script-taxonomy-only baseline measures what the full surfaces add beyond the API taxonomy.
- A matched real-vs-fork control bounds the intervention distribution shift: fork an ability that also resolved for real in the same game and compare predictions on the pair, since fork states are systematically odd (no stack, missing this-turn history).
- The linear-decodability battery from [`2026-08-28-encoder-preferences.md`](2026-08-28-encoder-preferences.md), run on pooled per-card `e` vectors against the gen-4 encoder: downstream consumers see `e` frozen, so what is linearly readable from it is the deliverable, and the ridge harness and feature table already exist.
- A pooled-`e` scorer smoke test: mean/max-pool a card's `e` vectors with the deterministic features into the existing `.npz` slot and re-run `train-scorer` Phase A on the same corpus. The fair variant concatenates pooled `e` with the current encoder's vector rather than swapping, because a mechanism-only vector deliberately lacks the winnability axis the scorer leans on, and a swap mostly measures that absence. Informational, not gating.
- The ward canary: cosine distance between ward and its spelled-out functional twins, which text similarity alone cannot close.
- Zero-shot keyword check: treat one implemented keyword as unknown (its token withheld from training, its occurrences always expanded to its definition) and measure effect-prediction quality on its records.
- Role-polarity probe: the model's predicted mana-pool sign for `{R}` in cost position vs effect position.
- Average-effect control: train the state-masked variant end to end, including its own encoder (that variant is exactly the average-effect regime this design argues against), and compare the two encoders' cached `e` on the `e`-side deliverables: the decodability battery, the scorer smoke test, the ward canary, scaling calibration. Masking state only at the effect head is not a test, because state obviously helps the head and that comparison cannot fail.
- Scaling calibration: a sweeper's predicted deaths as a function of board size, checking that state-conditioning is actually used rather than averaged over.
- Every gate in this design gets a numeric threshold in the spec before training starts: the identity-baseline margin, the damage-step keyword-separation canary that triggers the probe, and the collapse canaries. Gated decisions without stated criteria get renegotiated after the fact.

## Feasibility of the collectors — verified in the Forge source (2026-09-04 and 2026-09-05)

All of the record kinds are implementable against the Forge codebase as it stands, and none of the feasibility-gating questions forces a design change. Each subsection below states the mechanism its collector will use and the gaps that need their own instrumentation, from reads of the sibling Forge checkout on 2026-09-04 and 2026-09-05.

### Attributed events flow through one hookable funnel

Every mutation report in Forge funnels through `TriggerHandler.runTrigger`, and its `TriggerType` argument is a closed 145-member enum, so the event vocabulary already exists rather than needing invention. The parameter maps carry the causing `SpellAbility` under `AbilityKey.Cause` for the main categories: damage, zone changes (which cover deaths, draws, exiles, and tokens entering play), counters, and taps. A second hook brackets `MagicStack.resolveStack`, where the resolving ability is readable before resolution starts; the bracket supplies attribution for firing sites that drop the cause.

The gaps have known mechanisms rather than unknowns. The causeless list is longer than a handful: `LifeLost`, `Drawn`, `Untaps`, `Destroyed`, `TokenCreated`, the whole batched-damage family, and combat damage all carry no cause. Each either has a cause-bearing sibling (`ChangesZone` for draws and deaths) or falls inside a bracket (resolution, or the combat damage step). State-based-action deaths are causally anonymous in the engine, confirming the accepted approximation; the resolution bracket attributes them (nothing is pending when a resolution starts, so the pass that follows the bracket belongs to it), and the carried patch also adds the missing cause at the `Destroyed` firing site, where the ability is in scope but currently dropped.

Continuous power/toughness and keyword changes fire no trigger at all, which is correct per the rules but makes pump effects invisible to the trigger channel. Forge's event bus fires `GameEventCardStatsChanged` for exactly this category, and the resolution bracket supplies the attribution the bus lacks. That event carries current values rather than deltas, so the collector diffs computed stats inside the bracket and coalesces the burst an anthem's recomputation causes.

Three engine details temper the hook. Mana abilities never reach the stack (they resolve inline on activation, and one AI path resolves abilities with no stack at all), but the inline path fires the cast and resolution triggers with the ability in the parameters, so mana records ride the already-planned trigger hook and need no fourth patch. `CounterAdded` fires once per counter, so counter accounting reads the batched variant. And the `Cause` parameter is polymorphic and can be overwritten by a later bulk parameter copy at some firing sites, so the logger validates its type rather than trusting it.

The patch surface is three hooks, not one: the cause-carrying trigger channel (the trigger handler is private to `Game` with no injection point), the replacement execution point (also private), and a threaded pointer for the currently resolving sub-ability, which no engine field exposes. The bus channel needs no patch at all (`Game.subscribeToEvents` is public, and the resolving root ability is readable from the stack during resolution), but only four of its event records carry a spell ability, and as a view; degraded mode on stock Forge means bracket-only attribution. The trigger funnel is also not a mutation funnel: the bus has its own large set of firing sites and some mutations report on only one channel, so the event vocabulary is the union of trigger types, bus events, and bracket diffs, with an explicit per-effect-API completeness check. The patch is carried in the sibling checkout, which this project already builds locally as a hard dependency; a bytecode agent at worker startup was considered as the no-patch alternative and rejected as a heavier moving part than the small patch set it avoids. The cost is re-applying the patches on Forge updates.

### Runtime abilities join to converted lines by printed provenance, not by line ordinal

The converter's bracket numbers cannot serve as the join key. They run one per-face counter across five mixed line kinds, keyword-derived lines are emitted first in the file but appended last in Forge's runtime lists, and the converter merges, deduplicates, and splits abilities relative to the runtime objects. The stable key is printed provenance: script file, face, trait kind, and index within that kind's slice of the face's raw trait list. Both sides can produce it.

On the runtime side, every trait object reports its printed face, intrinsic-vs-granted status, and originating keyword (`getCardState()`, `isIntrinsic()`, `getKeyword()`, `getOriginalHost()`, `isCopiedTrait()`; `getGrantorStatic()` exists only on spell abilities and does not survive copies), so granted and copied abilities resolve to the donor card's printed line. Spell copies need fallbacks of their own: the original-ability back-reference is set only for copied abilities, a Fork-style spell copy carries only a copied flag, and for granted abilities the original-host accessor returns the affected card, so the granting aura or keyword is reached through the grantor accessors instead. The sidecar key computation carries those fallbacks.

On the converter side, the change is additive: the converter already holds the exact runtime object it renders each line from, and records that provenance in a sidecar metadata file paired with each converted card rather than as inline markers, so the ability text the tokenizer sees stays unchanged. A paired metadata file matches how converted cards already pair with their `.npz` embedding files. The sidecar also absorbs the awkward cases: a line the converter built from several runtime traits carries several provenance keys, and a trait whose line was deduplicated away maps to no line at all. Normalized-text matching was considered as a no-converter-change fallback and rejected, because it silently fails exactly where the converter merged or deduplicated lines.

### Fork-and-strip probes have working precedent in Forge's own machinery

`GameCopier` copies a live game and recomputes continuous effects on the copy rather than copying them, which is the probe's exact requirement, and Forge's simulation tests exercise the full copy-edit-recompute-read sequence. The two edit types sit below the layer system (`CardState.removeStaticAbility` for ability removal, `setBasePower`/`setBaseToughness` for printed characteristics), so they survive the `checkStaticAbilities` recomputation instead of being wiped by it. Combat legality and castability are queryable on a copy through static predicates, and forge-ai, where the copier lives, is already on every worker's classpath. The copy dominates probe cost, because it re-parses every card in the game from its script; Forge's own AI tolerates tens of copies per decision, so the per-game probe budget the design assumed is the right control, not a redesign.

Three risks are real and managed. The copier has an acknowledged fidelity backlog, and its own `ensureGameCopyScoreMatches` guard run on unperturbed copies detects drift. Edits must never touch the timestamped layer tables. And Forge's global random source cannot be snapshotted (it is a `SecureRandom`), so probe branches are made deterministic by installing a seeded generator and restoring the reference afterward, the engine's own simulation pattern.

Further copier facts shape collection. The stack and most this-turn history are not copied, so fork-based records store the fork's own state. Card ids are freshly assigned on the copy, so objects map through the copier's lookup, which throws on a miss, and tokens created on the fork map to nothing. Stripping a printed keyword needs an explicit keyword-cache refresh, or the has-keyword checks read stale state. Triggers are suppressed while the copier runs, so the event hook is silent during probe setup (desirable, and stated here so it isn't mistaken for a bug), and the copier's object-mapping helper throws on anything that is not a card or a player. The experimental `GameSnapshot` copier would avoid the per-card re-parse cost but ships with its static-effect handling disabled; `GameCopier` is the one to use. The AI's own seed-restore is not wrapped in a finally, so the collector's version must be, and two concurrent games in one JVM with probes on are forbidden, because the random source is process-global.

### Continuous-effect attribution is already in the engine

The per-card layer tables are keyed by (timestamp, static id) for keywords, types, colors, and names, and the continuous-effects pass writes every change with the applying static's id, so per-static per-entity attribution is a read, not an inference. Two limits verified in source: the applied-effect object does not expose its static ability, but the layer tables' column key is the public static id, so the join is a direct id read; and temporary pumps write static id zero into the same tables, which is why until-end-of-turn effects stay on the resolution bracket. For the record schema's sake, the replacement-type enum has 39 members to the trigger enum's 145.

### Keyword definitions are extractable for most keywords, with reminder templates for the rest

Roughly 150 of Forge's 199 keywords generate their desugared implementation as script text, and the capture point exists: the generated script strings are built as plain text in the keyword factory before being parsed, so the collector records them at generation rather than reconstructing them from parsed parameter maps, which alphabetize keys and detach sub-abilities. The engine-coded minority generates no script (ancient primitives like first strike, trample, deathtouch, lifelink, and haste, plus the cost-modifying family like kicker and convoke); for those, Forge carries per-keyword reminder-text templates that need no card at all. The split is benign for the zero-shot goal, because new-set variation keywords are precisely the script-generated kind, while the engine-coded set is old, stable, and densely trained. Generated definitions do reference other keywords (blitz grants haste; flying's static names reach), which expansion dropout absorbs: a referenced keyword stays a token that may itself expand on other samples. A few keywords (saga chapters, class levels) have only per-card definitions, because their effect bodies live on the host card's variables.

## Build order — first embeddings before full machinery

The subsystems stage so that the first `e` vectors exist and pass canaries as early as possible. Stage one needs no Forge patch at all: the event bus plus the resolution bracket, bracket-attributed resolution records and combat records, the provenance sidecar, and the trainer with per-entity heads, the script-API classification auxiliary, and keyword-expansion dropout over reminder-text definitions (a training-time augmentation needing no collector). That is enough for the nearest-neighbor, ward, and damage-step keyword-separation canaries. Combat sits in stage one because the damage-step keywords have no behavioral signal without it; the canary is scoped to that family, since evasion keywords have no combat-record gradient and their non-separation at stage one is expected, not a failure. Mana-ability records also wait for the patch, since the bus-only stage cannot see the inline mana path. Stage two is the three-hook patch set and what it unlocks (cause-attributed triggers, mana records, rewrite records, sub-ability attribution), plus playability records (which bring the evasion keywords' signal), continuous-effect and trigger-fire records, and the coverage collector for the fifth of the corpus match play never reaches. Stage three adds interventional resolutions and, only if the damage-step canary fails, the probe. Stage four is the script surface: vocabulary rebuild, the compositional script tokenizer, the asymmetric paired-encoding loss, the definition upgrade from reminder text to captured scripts, and synthetic script variants. Each stage widens the corpus without invalidating earlier records, since the record schema is fixed up front.

## Open questions

- Event-record field layout: the vocabulary is the union of trigger types, bus events, and bracket diffs; which members the corpus keeps, the per-type field normalization, and the per-effect-API completeness check are spec work.
- Multi-task balancing: sampling ratios and per-kind loss normalization across record kinds whose frequencies differ by orders of magnitude, plus per-entity target sparsity (most entities are unaffected by most records) and per-record normalization over entity count.
- Pump-effect channel details: which `GameEventCardStatsChanged` payloads reconstruct stat and keyword deltas, and how to deduplicate against the trigger channel.
- Definition synthesis parameters: representative parameter values per keyword class when instantiating definitions for expansion, and handling of keywords whose definitions exist only per card (saga chapters, class levels).
- Budgets and rates: per-game caps for interventional resolutions (and damage-step probes if built), counterfactual targets per real resolution, and sampling rates for playability and continuous-effect records, which fire far more often than the corpus needs.
