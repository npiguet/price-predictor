# May 26, 2026 — Picker top-k objective tuning

**TL;DR:** The first real `train-picker` run exposed two bugs
(NaN gradients from a `torch.where`-with-`-inf` trap, and 18 GB
of redundant embeddings) that Claude traced and fixed. After
validating that training now works, I spent the session analysing
training results, debating REINFORCE mechanics, and ultimately
implementing a top-k reward-ranked objective to push past the
REINFORCE plateau.

The day started with the picker crashing on the first batch due to a
CUDA device-side assert. Claude isolated the root cause by running
training steps on CPU and inspecting per-term gradient finiteness:
the entropy loss produced NaN gradients in every parameter, even
though the forward value was finite. The culprit was `_policy_entropy`
computing `p * logp` at masked positions where `logp = -inf` and
`p = 0` — `torch.where` hides `0 * -inf = NaN` from the forward
pass but autograd still propagates the NaN backward. `_kl_penalty`
had the same flaw. The fix was to zero the `-inf` log-probs *before*
the product rather than masking the result after it. This meant the
picker had never actually trained before — the crash happened on the
very first batch of every prior run.

The memory issue was separate: 100k pools were each storing a dense
copy of their card embeddings, totalling about 18 GB. Switching to a
shared embedding table (27,948 unique cards, 61 MB) with per-pool
integer indices cut the footprint to roughly 130 MB.

Once training was stable, I watched the first real run through 41
epochs. `val_reward` climbed from 1.96 to 2.23 before early stopping.
I noted that the deck-shape sanity metrics — ~2.9 colors, ~18
creatures, a front-loaded CMC curve — looked reasonable from the very
start, and drew the conclusion that the cold-start worry for
REINFORCE from random initialization had not been realized: there was
enough within-pool score variance across 23-card subsets to produce
non-zero advantages.

I raised a question about whether decaying the number of samples over
time would sharpen the signal late in training. Claude explained why
that backfires: the 64 samples are a variance-reduction tool for the
gradient estimate, not a sharpness knob. Cutting samples would
degrade the per-pool baseline estimate, eventually approaching zero
gradient at `n_samples = 1`. The correct sharpening mechanism is
entropy or temperature annealing, and the entropy schedule was already
in place — though Claude flagged that it had never actually engaged,
because the "5 consecutive val-reward improvements" trigger condition
was never met against the noisy reward curve.

I also pushed back on Claude's repeated caveat that forge-best's low
scorer score is "somewhat circular." I pointed out that the scorer is
trained on the outcomes of matches that include forge-built decks, so
a low score genuinely predicts a low win rate — forge is in-
distribution. Claude conceded the point, and I had it save that
correction to memory so it would stop repeating the caveat.

The day then shifted to the gen-4 match-play evaluation results,
which I pasted into the session. The ranking is monotonic across all
five builder variants: gen4-512 (57.8% Bo7) > gen4-256 (54.4%) >
gen3-256 (53.0%) > gen3-128 (47.6%) > forge-best (38.2%). Gen4-512
beats gen4-256 at ~3.3σ, which overturns the val_acc-based decision
from the gen-4 scorer sweep that had shelved 512d as unjustified.
Claude confirmed that match independence holds (decks are not reused
across matches), so the sigmas are firm. I had these conclusions
recorded in the existing gen-4 experiment file in report style.

I also added a conclusion that a gen-5 of the same architecture is
unlikely to move match play measurably. Claude worked out that the
gen3-to-gen4 transition at the same width had already bundled three
levers — a new encoder retrain, +70% more match data, and margin
weighting — for only a ~2.4σ head-to-head improvement, while the
single width change (256d to 512d) delivered more at ~3.3σ. The
implication is that more same-architecture self-play data has
diminishing returns from here; the higher-yield path would be
structural.

After that I turned back to improving the picker. Three attempts
failed: advantage normalization (GRPO-style per-pool std
normalization) caused the policy to collapse to near-uniform by epoch
3–4 because the rescaled gradient was far too large relative to the
entropy bonus; dropout at 0.1 caused entropy to spike to near-maximum
(~4.19 nats for a ~90-card pool) and the policy to produce random
decks, because on-policy REINFORCE cannot tolerate dropout noise
injected directly into the policy.

Claude explained why the supervised-vs-RL boundary matters for
dropout: the scorer is trained with fixed targets so dropout is a
clean regularizer, but the picker's training targets are generated by
the dropout-perturbed policy itself, so dropout corrupts the very
signal the picker learns from. I accepted this and moved on.

The alternative Claude proposed was a top-k reward-ranked objective
(related to RAFT/ReST): sample 64 decks per pool, keep the top-k by
scorer reward, and train with plain maximum likelihood on those kept
decks. This avoids the tiny-advantage problem that plagues REINFORCE
here (64 decks per pool score very similarly, so advantages are ~0.01
and the gradient is weak), removes the negative gradient term that
drives instability, and gives a stable supervised-style objective. I
decided to implement it as an opt-in `--objective topk` flag.

I then asked whether I could resume from the existing 2.234
checkpoint rather than starting from scratch. Claude confirmed this is
legitimate — it is essentially the "improve" step of ReST, which
normally warm-starts from a pretrained policy. The flag was wired as
resumable rather than architecture-locked.

A warm-start run with defaults (lr=3e-4) showed that top-k does push
past the 2.234 baseline, peaking at 2.281, but entropy collapsed fast
to ~0.5–0.7 nats. A second attempt with `--lr 1e-4 --entropy-coef
0.03 --temperature 1.2` kept entropy at ~1.7–1.9 nats and was still
climbing when I last looked (epoch 36 at 2.24), though still within
the same ~2.24–2.28 band.

To get a sense of the ceiling, I had Claude run a quick one-shot
scoring of the gen4-512 builder's 10,000 decks. The builder's mean
score is 2.36, median 2.44, with most decks in the 1.0–3.7 range.
The picker at ~2.28 sits in the middle of that distribution, roughly
0.08 below the builder mean. That difference, at the pipeline's
calibration of ~7.8 pp per 1.0 score gap, maps to about half a
percentage point of win rate — a narrow margin that suggests limited
remaining headroom.
